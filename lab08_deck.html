1.1 · New Method: Histograms on the Density Scale

Plotting a Distribution with Its Mean

  • plots.hist(df['col']) draws a histogram of one column.
  • density=True puts the bars on the density scale: the total area is 1, so the heights are small decimals, not counts. That is why the lab sets plots.ylim to values like 0.07.
  • bins= sets the bin edges. np.arange(a, b, step) is a handy way to write them.
  • plots.scatter(x, 0, color='red', s=50) puts a dot on the horizontal axis at x, such as the mean.
Example with a made-up table
plots.hist(students['commute'], density=True,
           bins=np.arange(0, 60, 5))

plots.scatter(np.mean(students['commute']), 0,
              color='red', zorder=2, s=50)

plots.ylim(-0.005, 0.05);

1.1 · Picture

Where the Mean and Median Sit

▬ mean   ▬▬ median (the dashed blue line)

Left-skewed
The long tail is on the left. The mean is pulled toward the tail, so it tends to sit below the median.
Symmetric
No long tail. The mean and the median are equal.
Right-skewed
The long tail is on the right. The mean is pulled toward the tail, so it tends to sit above the median.

The mean is the balance point of the distribution. The median is the halfway point of the data.

1.2 · New Method: Marking One SD

The SD and Marking It on a Plot

  • The SD describes, roughly, how far values tend to be from the mean. It is the root mean square of the deviations.
  • np.std(arr) computes it. Use np.mean(arr) for the mean.
  • To mark one SD above and below the mean, plot two more dots at mean + sd and mean - sd.
  • marker='^' makes a triangle instead of a circle, so the SD marks look different from the mean.
Example with made-up values
m = np.mean(values)
sd = np.std(values)

plots.scatter(m, 0, color='red', s=50)
plots.scatter(m + sd, 0, marker='^',
              color='blue', s=50)
plots.scatter(m - sd, 0, marker='^',
              color='blue', s=50)

1.3 · Picture

The Normal Curve and the SD

  • A normal (bell-shaped) curve is symmetric and centered at the mean.
  • The red dots are the points of inflection, where the curve changes from bending downward to bending upward. Each is about one SD from the mean.
  • For a roughly normal distribution, one SD from the mean falls near these points. For a skewed one, the SD is hard to spot on the plot.

Bridge to Section 2

The Central Limit Theorem

  • The distribution of the sums or means of large random samples, drawn with replacement from a population, is roughly normal, whatever the shape of the population.
  • It describes the distribution of a statistic (here, the sample mean). It does not describe the original data.
  • How a statistic varies across many repeated samples is its sampling distribution.
  • Section 2 explores how the SD of these sample means depends on the sample size.

Section 2 · The Setup

In This Lab, the Data Is the Population

  • The table of 2023 San Francisco city salaries is treated as the whole population. Real populations are rarely available, but here it lets us see exactly how sample means behave.
  • Its distribution is skewed. It is clearly not normal.
  • We draw many random samples with replacement from this population, compute each sample's mean, and look at the distribution of those sample means.
  • The point is to see what the CLT says: even from a skewed population, the sample means form a bell shape.

Sampling Rows for the Sample Mean

2.8 · Proportional Relationships

Proportional and Inversely Proportional

  • If y is directly proportional to x, then multiplying x by a number multiplies y by the same number.
  • If y is inversely proportional to x, then multiplying x by a number divides y by that number.
  • The same idea works with a square root: “proportional to √x” means y changes along with √x.
A made-up example
np.sqrt(4)    # 2.0
np.sqrt(16)   # 4.0
np.sqrt(100)  # 10.0

Closing Note

Population vs. a Single Sample (the Bootstrap)

  • In this lab we sampled from the population. In practice you have one sample, and the bootstrap resamples from that sample instead.
  • The same principles hold. A larger original sample gives resampled means with a smaller SD.
  • The histogram of bootstrap means is centered on the original sample mean, not on the population mean, which you usually do not know.
  • To change the variability of the sample mean, you must change the size of the original sample.
