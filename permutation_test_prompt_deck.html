Background

About the Data

  • The data come from a sample of newborns in a large hospital system. The textbook treats it as a simple random sample, even though the sampling was done in multiple stages.
  • There are 1,174 mother-baby pairs: 459 mothers who smoked during pregnancy and 715 who did not.
  • For each pair, the table records: the baby's birth weight (ounces), gestational days, the mother's age (years), height (inches), pregnancy weight (pounds), and whether the mother smoked during pregnancy.
  • More detail about a larger version of this data set is in Stat Labs by Deborah Nolan and Terry Speed.
Background

What the Study Was Asking

  • One aim of the study was to see whether maternal smoking was associated with birth weight. In the textbook, babies of mothers who smoked weighed about 9 ounces less on average in this sample, and a permutation test supported that difference.
  • The textbook then uses the same data and the same method to compare other attributes of the two groups, such as the mothers' ages.
  • In this activity you ask that different question: mothers' ages.
Prompt

Were Mothers Who Smoked Younger?

  • Researchers studied 1,174 mother-baby pairs at a large hospital system. 459 of the mothers smoked during pregnancy and 715 did not.
  • Using baby.csv (columns Maternal Age and Maternal Smoker), test whether mothers who smoked were younger on average than mothers who did not.
  • Report your analysis following the four steps from the textbook: the hypotheses, the test statistic, the distribution under the null, and the conclusion.
  • Hint: remember that association is not causation. Think about what you are actually comparing: the ages of two groups of mothers.
Deliverables

What to Turn In

  1. 1
    Hypotheses. The null and the alternative, in words.
  2. 2
    Test statistic. How it is calculated, which sign favors the alternative, and its observed value.
  3. 3
    Simulation. Your code and a histogram of at least 5,000 simulated statistics, with the observed value marked.
  4. 4
    P-value and conclusion. The p-value, your decision at the 5% cutoff, and what it means in plain language.
  5. 5
    Cause. One or two sentences on what the result does and does not show.
